# Evidence — Claim Strength by Source Tier

Every claim carries the strength of its weakest source. State the tier; never
let a weak source wear a strong claim.

## Process

1. **Tag the source tier** for each fact before you use it:
   - **Primary** — original data, measurements, reproducible experiments.
   - **Peer-reviewed** — vetted by the field.
   - **Secondary** — reputable review/synthesis, one step from primary.
   - **Anecdotal / single-source** — one opinion, one vendor, one unverified
     report.
2. **Match claim strength to tier.** A secondary source supports "X is
   associated with Y", not "X causes Y".
3. **Separate the proven from the likely from the unknown.** Three buckets, no
   blurring.

## Anti-Rationalization

- "It's probably true" → name the tier. "Probably" without a tier is a guess
  wearing a lab coat.
- "Multiple blogs said it" → multiple anecdotes are still anecdotal.
- "The vendor's whitepaper" → a vendor source serves the vendor; tier it down.

## Evidence

- A claim is well-grounded when its highest-cited source tier matches the claim's
  asserted strength, and weaker tiers are labeled as such.

## Red Flags

- Causal language ("causes", "proves") resting on correlational or single-source
  evidence.
- Treating a reputable name as proof (argument from authority).
- One source, stated as settled consensus.
